Owners Corporation 1 PS No. 834726V v Melbourne Owners Corporation Services Pty Ltd (Owners Corporations) [2024] VCAT 199 (5 March 2024)

Last Updated: 13 March 2024

VICTORIAN CIVIL AND ADMINISTRATIVE TRIBUNAL

CIVIL DIVISION

OWNERS CORPORATIONS LIST VCAT REFERENCE NO. OC211/2023

CATCHWORDS
Section 67B(1) Owners Corporations Act 2006 (Vic); Sections 122A and 122(d) Owners Corporations Act 2006 (Vic) breach of duties of manager.

APPLICANT
Owners Corporation 1 PS No. 834726V
RESPONDENT
Melbourne Owners Corporation Services Pty Ltd ACN 164 870 464
WHERE HELD
Melbourne
BEFORE
Member L Rowland
HEARING TYPE
Hearing
DATE OF HEARING
23 February 2024
DATE OF ORDER
5 March 2024
CITATION
Owners Corporation 1 PS No. 834726V v Melbourne Owners Corporation Services Pty Ltd (Owners Corporations) [2024] VCAT 199

ORDER

  1. The Tribunal declares that the meeting held on 11 July 2022 was void for failure to comply with the 14-day mandatory notice period under section 72 of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 (Vic).
  2. The Tribunal declares that the contract of appointment dated 11 July 2022 expired on 10 November 2022 at the first annual general meeting.
  3. The respondent must now pay the applicant $1,769.94 plus interest of $177.41 and $67.40 reimbursement of the Tribunal application fee, a total of $2,014.75.



L Rowland

Member



APPEARANCES:

For applicant
Mr Kingma, secretary
For respondent
Mr Shawn Lu, general manager

REASONS

Introduction

  1. The owners corporation seeks recovery of approximately $2,400 it says was illegally removed from its bank account by the respondent (“MOCS”), after its contract of appointment expired. MOCS says its appointment was illegally terminated and it was entitled to take the funds from the owners corporation's account.
  2. The dispute concerns the operation of section 67B(1) of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 (Vic) (“the Act”) which was enacted as part of the 2021 reforms. Section 67B(1) provides:

If the applicant for registration of the plan of subdivision appoints a third party manager prior to the first meeting of the owners corporation, the contract of appointment of the third party manager expires at that first meeting.

  1. The owners corporation says MOCS’ appointment expired at the first meeting of new lot owners on 10 November 2022. MOCS says that the meeting on 10 November 2022 was the second annual general meeting of the owners corporation and so its contract of appointment did not expire.

Background

  1. The owners corporation was created on 6 July 2022, when the plan of subdivision for the 4-lot development was registered with the Registrar of Titles.
  2. On 11 July 2022, the developer and initial owner purported to hold the first meeting of the owners corporation under section 66 of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 (Vic). Section 66 requires the meeting to be conducted within 6 months of registration of the plan. The purpose of the meeting is for the developer to provide the documents pertaining to the owners corporation to the owners corporation. As noted on the Consumer Affairs Victoria website “The initial owner is usually the owners corporation’s sole member at this stage.” Typically, developers hold the first meeting as soon as possible after registration of the plan to pass the burden of insuring the building under section 9AAA of the Sale of Land Act 1962 (Vic) to the owners corporation. At the first meeting, a manager is usually appointed, insurance effected or transferred, and a budget approved so that fees can be levied immediately. The owners corporation fees are then adjusted at settlement which takes place within a few weeks after registration of the plan.
  3. At the meeting on 11 July 2022, MOCS was appointed for a 3-year term, the maximum term permitted under s 119(1D) of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 (Vic).
  4. On 12 July 2022, MOCS, on behalf of the owners corporation levied fees for the 6-month period ending 31 December 2022. On 13 July 2022, the developer paid the owners corporation fees. The four lots settled on or shortly after 20 July 2022. The owners corporation fees were adjusted as part of the settlement of the lots. The owners corporation insurance was in place since May 2022 and separately adjusted at settlement.
  5. In September 2022, MOCS, arranged for the nature strip at the subdivision to be mowed. In September or October 2022, the gardeners put up a MOCS sign. Mr Kingma, who represented the owners corporation at the hearing, said this was the first time the new lot owners became aware of MOCS’ existence.
  6. In October 2022, Mr Kingma requested MOCS to arrange a general meeting of the owners corporation.
  7. On 10 November 2022, a general meeting was held at Mr Kingma’s lot. A representative from MOCS was in attendance. The meeting resolved to “terminate” MOCS’ services with the owners self-managing the owners corporation.
  8. Mr Kingma believed that section 67B(1) of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 (Vic) operated so that the term of MOCS’ appointment automatically expired at the meeting on 10 November 2022. Mr Kingma was fortified in his view by a 2021 Consumer Affairs Victoria Fact Sheet entitled “Summary of Amendments Affecting Developers” which is published on the Consumer Affairs Victoria website. The fact sheet states:

Restrictions on contracts entered into by the Developer

The Act introduces the following constraints on contracts entered into by a developer:

  • If the developer appoints an OC manager who is neither the developer or a lot owner prior to the first meeting of the owners corporation, the contract of appointment of the OC manager expires at the first meeting.
  • If the developer enters into any other contract (other than a contract of appointment for an OC manager) that relates to the OC and benefits the developer, that contract term must not exceed 3 years in duration. However, this restriction will not apply to hotel and resort management contracts.

These changes mean that developers cannot enter into long-term contracts to their own benefit at the expense of the OC, while empowering lot owners to appoint managers and other service providers on terms which represent their best interests. Note of course that there is nothing preventing a developer from putting forward their preferred contractor(s) for consideration at the first meeting.

  1. On 20 November 2022, Mr Kingma emailed MOCS advising that the owners corporation had passed a resolution to terminate the contract with MOCS relying on section 67B(1) of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 (Vic).
  2. On 22 November 2022, Mr Shawn Lu from MOCS responded to the effect that section 67B(1) did not permit the new lot owners to terminate the contract. Mr Lu noted that at the first meeting of the owners corporation on 11 July 2022 MOCS was appointed. It was not appointed prior to the first meeting so 67B(1) did not apply. Mr Lu then proceeded to outline MOCS’ claim for damages for unlawful breach of contract of appointment. Mr Lu asked Mr Kingma, to confirm that the owners corporation intended to continue with the unlawful termination in light of MOCS’ damages claim.
  3. Three years is a long time to be bound by a contract of appointment, if the owners corporation neither needs nor wants the manager’s services.
  4. On 6 December 2022, Mr Kingma confirmed the owners corporation’s intention to discontinue with MOCS’ services. MOCS’ accepted this as the effective date of notification of termination rather than accepting the ratified notification of 20 November 2022. MOCS calculated its entitlement to fees to 5 January 2023 and compensation to 10 July 2025.
  5. On 5 January 2023 MOCS returned $171.70 to the owners corporation having deducted the following amounts from the owners corporation’s funds:

Management fees at the rate of $91.67 per month from 11 July through to 5 January 2023 $641.69

Disbursements at the rate of $16.67 per month from 11 July through to 5 January 2023 $100.02

Handover fee $550

Maintenance - Lawns and Gardening $99 per month for 4 months $396

Additional services - attendance at Meeting on 10 November $330

Admin Set up costs $150

Damages for exiting contract early $827.26

  1. On 23 February 2023, the owners corporation commenced these proceedings.

Did the manager’s contract expire under section 67B(1) on 10 November 2022?

  1. MOCS contended that its contract did not expire because the meeting on 10 November 2022 was not the first annual general meeting but the second annual general meeting. Section 70(1) provides:

The first meeting of an owners corporation convened under section 66 is the first annual general meeting.

  1. Mr Kingma said he had great difficulty reconciling the Consumer Affairs Victoria fact sheet on the amendments to the Act with the wording of the Act.
  2. Under the Act, the first meeting under section 66 is also the first annual general meeting. It is typically only attended by the developer, so there is no opportunity for lot owners to appoint their manager as stated in the Consumer Affairs Victoria Fact Sheet.
  3. If the meeting on 10 November 2022 is the second annual general meeting, the contract of appointment could not have expired under section 67B(1). It could have only expired at the first meeting with the developer and only if the developer had entered into the contract of appointment prior to the first meeting.
  4. Mr Kingma argued that because the first meeting was also an annual general meeting the developer was required to comply with the mandatory 14-day notice period under section 72 of the Act. Mr Kingma contended that the first annual general meeting could not be held within the first 14 days of registration of the plan and the meeting on 11 July 2022 is void. Section 72(1) provides:

The person convening an annual general meeting must give notice in writing of the meeting to each lot owner at least 14 days before the meeting.

  1. Mr Lu contended that the short notice was a technical breach of the Act and that I should not make an order invalidating the meeting of 11 July 2022 because as a matter of practicality the same decision would have been made if 14 days’ notice had been given and the developer was only giving notice to itself. I was referred to my decision in Jenkins v OCVM Commercial Pty Ltd (Owners Corporations) [2019] VCAT 1078. I will set out what I said in Jenkins here (footnotes excluded).

What is the effect of a breach of the Act?

  1. The Owners Corporations Act 2006 does not provide for a consequence for a breach of the notice and quorum sections of Part 4 of the Act. It does not specifically state that anything done, or any decision made in breach of the sections is void. It is therefore necessary to consider the proper construction of the sections to determine the effect on a decision made in breach of the sections.
  2. The New South Wales Court of Appeal considered a similar issue in two recent decisions involving the Strata Schemes Management Act 1996 (NSW) (since repealed).
  3. In both cases the Court of Appeal found that the failure to comply with the Strata Schemes Management Act 1996 (NSW) did not invalidate the decisions made by the owners corporations.
  4. The first was 2 Elizabeth Bay Road Pty Ltd v The Owners – Strata Plan No 73943 [2014] NSWCA 409 in which the owners corporation commenced a legal proceeding in the absence of the required resolution of lot owners. The Court of Appeal determined that the failure to obtain the resolution did not render the proceedings void. The Victorian Supreme Court came to the same conclusion in Burbank Australia Pty Ltd v Owners Corporation PS447493.
  5. The second decision was The Owners Strata Plan No 57164 v Yau [2017] NSWCA 341 which concerned a committee decision to settle a legal proceeding involving the Yaus. A committee meeting was called at short notice in the middle of a trial. The committee authorised a large sum in settlement of the Yaus’ claim. A week after consent orders were filed with the Court, the Court of Appeal handed down its decision in The Owners Strata Plan 50276 v Thoo. The Thoo decision seriously called in question the liability of the owners corporation to pay anything to the Yaus. By subsequent proceedings, the owners corporation sought to set aside the consent orders. The owners corporation claimed that the committee meeting authorising the settlement sum was void because insufficient notice of the meeting was given. The Court of Appeal found that insufficient notice in breach of the Act did not invalidate the meeting or decisions made at that meeting.
  6. The NSW Court of Appeal applied the well-established principles of statutory construction to the Strata Scheme Management Act 1996. The Court of Appeal found that the purposes of the Act were not achieved by invalidating the decisions made in breach of the Act.
  7. Potentially, there are two breaches of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 in this proceeding; a failure to give 14 days’ notice of the ballot and a failure to achieve a quorum. There is no stated consequence for the breaches. Specifically, it is not expressly stated that any decision made in breach of the Act is void. Only two sections in Part 4 of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 provide a consequence for a breach of the relevant section. Section 87(7) provides that a manager’s contract of appointment is voidable if made in contravention of section 87(4) and section 89 provides for a penalty of up to 60 penalty units for demanding a power of attorney or proxy.
  8. It is necessary to interpret the Owners Corporations Act 2006 to determine the consequence of a failure to comply with the Act where no consequence is stated.
  9. The principles of statutory construction were conveniently summarised by the Court of Appeal in David Joseph v Jayne Worthington & Anor by quoting Derham AsJ as follows (footnotes excluded):
    1. Derham As J summarised the relevant principles of statutory construction succinctly and accurately by reference to High Court authority. They are encapsulated in the following statements.
    2. In Alcan (NT) Alumina Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Territory Revenue, Hayne, Heydon, Crennan and Kiefel JJ emphasised the centrality of the words of the relevant statutory provision:

This Court has stated on many occasions that the task of statutory construction must begin with a consideration of the text itself. Historical considerations and extrinsic materials cannot be relied on to displace the clear meaning of the text. The language which has actually been employed in the text of legislation is the surest guide to legislative intention. The meaning of the text may require consideration of the context, which includes the general purpose and policy of a provision, in particular the mischief it is seeking to remedy.

  1. In Commissioner of Taxation v Consolidated Media Holdings Ltd, French CJ, Hayne, Crennan, Bell and Gageler JJ said:

This Court has stated on many occasions that the task of statutory construction must begin with a consideration of the [statutory] text. So must the task of statutory construction end. The statutory text must be considered in its context. That context includes legislative history and extrinsic materials. Understanding context has utility if, and in so far as, it assists in fixing the meaning of the statutory text. Legislative history and extrinsic materials cannot displace the meaning of the statutory text. Nor is their examination an end in itself.

  1. In Project Blue Sky Inc v Australian Broadcasting Authority, McHugh, Gummow, Kirby and Hayne JJ emphasised the importance of reading a statute as a whole:

The primary object of statutory construction is to construe the relevant provision so that it is consistent with the language and purpose of all the provisions of the statute. The meaning of the provision must be determined ‘by reference to the language of the instrument viewed as a whole’. In Commissioner for Railways (NSW) v Agalianos, Dixon CJ pointed out that ‘the context, the general purpose and policy of a provision and its consistency and fairness are surer guides to its meaning than the logic with which it is constructed’. Thus, the process of construction must always begin by examining the context of the provision that is being construed.

  1. Their Honours also endorsed the subsidiary principle that a court should strive to give meaning to every word of a statutory provision.
  2. One of the main purposes of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 is to provide for the management, powers and functions of owners corporations. The following sections require consideration in this proceeding:
    1. Sections 86(2) and 77 which ensure minimum participation of lot owners in a ballot so that any resolution of the owners corporation properly reflects the will of the lot owners;
    2. Section 94(1) which disentitles non-financial members from voting on ordinary resolutions to encourage lot owners to pay their fees and to give paying lot owners a greater say in how the owners corporation operates;
    3. The notice provision under section 84 which ensures lot owners are given proper notice of a ballot, to enable lot owners a reasonable opportunity to vote.
  3. The provisions of Part 4 of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 relate to the process of meetings and decisions of owners corporations. I do not perceive a legislative purpose to invalidate a decision of an owners corporation made in breach of Part 4 of the Act. Instead, I perceive a legislative purpose to set out a framework for volunteer committees and lot owners. I perceive a legislative purpose to provide a fair and transparent process to give lot owners the opportunity to participate in the decision-making process of the owners corporation. I do not consider invalidating every decision made in breach of the Act serves the purpose of Part 4. Where Part 4 intends for a specific consequence for a breach, it sets it out, as it did for sections 87 and 89 of the Act. If it were intended that any breach, of any section, would render a decision void, the Act would have expressly stated so.
  4. In my opinion, the Act does not provide a consequence for a breach of the provisions of Part 4 (apart from sections 87 and 89), because the Act recognises that a breach may be substantial or trifling. Each breach needs to be examined in its own context to determine what remedy, if any, is fair.
  5. Instead of providing a consequence for a breach, the Act provides that an aggrieved person, entitled to do so, may make a complaint to the Owners Corporation under Part 10 of the Act and/or apply to the Tribunal for any of the remedies set out under section 165 of the Act.
  6. The Tribunal may make any order it considers fair but must act within well-established principles of law. Not every breach will justify a remedy. In deciding whether an order should be made the Tribunal may have regard to; the effect on the outcome of anything done in breach of the Act; whether any lot owner suffered prejudice; whether the decision has been ratified; the impact on third parties and any other relevant matter.
  7. In conclusion, a meeting, resolution or decision made in breach of a provision of Part 4 of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 does not automatically render it void and of no effect. Instead, an aggrieved person, entitled to do so under section 163 of the Act, may apply to the Tribunal for a discretionary remedy. The Tribunal will need to carefully consider the circumstances of each case to determine if a remedy is appropriate and fair.
  8. I am unable to discern parliament’s intention for the operation of section 67B(1) when read with sections 70(1) and 72(1). In practice, section 67B(1) has no work to do. If there has been a drafting error, then it is a matter for parliament to legislate an amendment. In the meantime, I propose to give the legislation its plain meaning and enforce it accordingly. The meeting on 11 July 2022 was subject to a mandatory notice period of 14 days which was not complied with. I declare that the first annual general meeting on 11 July 2022 void. I would otherwise decline to exercise a discretion to validate the meeting for the following reasons:
(a) there is no good reason why the developer has not complied with the mandatory 14-day notice period,

(b) the developer had 6 months to hold the meeting,

(c) there was no imperative to hold the meeting within 14 days of registration, the insurance had been arranged and was adjusted separately to the owners corporation fees,

(d) there is no detriment to the owners corporation and lot owners in declaring the meeting void,

(e) MOCS who opposes an order invalidating the meeting does not come to the Tribunal as an innocent bystander,

  1. as an experienced owners corporation manager, it would or should have known the mandatory 14-day time period for the holding of the annual general meeting had not been met,
  2. rather than comply with the spirit of the amended legislation and allow the lot owners a choice in its owners corporation manager, it has relied on the wording of the Act to say that its contract of appointment has not expired,

iii. MOCS negotiated a 3-year contract with the developer in its own commercial interest,

  1. MOCS has otherwise not complied with the Act as will become evident later in these reasons.
  1. Having found that the meeting of 11 July 2022 is void, it follows that the annual general meeting on 10 November 2022 was the first annual general meeting. Therefore, section 67B(1) applied to MOCS’ contract of appointment. It expired on 10 November 2022. MOCS was not reappointed by the meeting.

Reimbursement of management fees, disbursements and handover fee of $550

  1. Mr Kingma sought reimbursement of some of the management fees and disbursements and the handover fee of $550 on the grounds that the new lot owners did not agree to the contract of appointment and the fees were excessive. The new lot owners were not provided with a copy of the contract of appointment until after proceedings had commenced. Even though the annual general meeting on 11 July was void, the developer, as 100% lot owner, was competent to enter into the contract of appointment with MOCS at any time. It was not necessary to enter into the contract of appointment at a meeting. The contract appointment entered into with MOCS on 11 July 2022 is binding on the owners corporation. I am satisfied that MOCS is entitled to the monthly management fees and disbursements but only to the date of expiration of the contract of appointment. All administrative fees and disbursements charged after 10 November 2022 must be refunded.
  2. Mr Kingma contended that the handover fee only applied when the books and records were being handed over to another manager. The handover fee is an agreed contractual sum payable on transfer of management to either another manager or to the self-managed owners corporation. MOCS was entitled to the contracted handover fee.
  3. MOCS must reimburse the owners corporation the management fees and disbursements charged after 10 November 2022. The total amount to be refunded is $216.68.

Reimbursement of lawn and gardening expenses $396

  1. Mr Kingma made three complaints with the lawn and gardening expenses charged to the owners corporation. First, Mr Kingma and the other lot owners were unaware of MOCS’ existence until gardeners arrived unannounced in or about September or October 2022. Mr Kingma said it would have been reasonable for MOCS, knowing that the lot owners had recently settled their properties, to send an introductory letter and asking what services the lot owners required rather than imposing services on the owners corporation. The common property is a concrete driveway with a newly planted garden bed which did not require any maintenance. The owners corporation does not have any lawns, but there is a council nature strip.
  2. The owners resolved to hold a meeting to better understand the role of MOCS. Mr Kingma said that was MOCS opportunity to present the management agreement, explain their services and seek instructions from the lot owners. Had MOCS enquired of the lot owners they would have told MOCS that no gardening services were required because the lot owners would be attending to the nature strip themselves.
  3. The second complaint regarding the gardening services is that the gardening services were carried out by Leon’s Property Maintenance & Care Pty Ltd (“Leon”) an entity associated with MOCS. The sole director of MOCS and the sole director of Leon are husband and wife. Under s 122A of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 (Vic) the manager was required to disclose by written notice to the chairperson of the owners corporation the beneficial relationship before any contract with Leon was entered into. Mr Kingma said that MOCS did not make the required disclosure.
  4. The third complaint is that MOCS, in breach of section 122(d) of the Act, failed to take reasonable steps to ensure the lawn mowing services were procured at a competitive price. Mr Kingma said that he obtained a telephone quotation from Jim’s Mowing to mow the nature strip for $30 compared with the $99 charged by Leon.
  5. Mr Lu said that MOCS disclosed the beneficial relationship to the owners corporation in the meeting of 11 July 2022. The minutes record the following:

Disclosure of Beneficial Relationships with Suppliers

Members present were advised of the beneficial relationship between Melbourne Owners Corporation Services Pty Ltd and the suppliers being Leon’s Property Maintenance & Care Pty Ltd and Asset Building Management Pty Ltd, where both are owned by the same sole director. Melbourne Owners Corporation Services Pty Ltd does not benefit directly from the suppliers, however there is a personal relationship (marriage) between the Directors of the two companies.

  1. Mr Lu said there was no formal contract entered into between the owners corporation and Leon, but rather a work order was issued to Leon to carry out the gardening work in September 2022.
  2. MOCS’ duty of disclosure is set out in section 122A(2) & (3) of the Act, which provides:
(2) The manager of an owners corporation must disclose any beneficial relationship with a supplier with whom a contract is proposed to be entered into for the supply of goods or services to the owners corporation.

(3) Disclosure required under subsection (2) must-

(a) be given by written notice to the chairperson of the owners corporation; and

(b) subject to subsection (4), be given

(i) immediately upon the manager becoming aware that the proposed contract is with a supplier with whom the manager has a beneficial relationship; and

(ii) before the contract is entered into by the owners corporation.

  1. MOCS’ duty to ensure a competitive price is obtained is set out in 122(d) of the Act which provides:

A manager

(d) must take reasonable steps to ensure than any goods or services procured by the manager on behalf of the owners corporation are procured at competitive prices and on competitive terms.
  1. The disclosure requirement under section 122A(3) is to give written notice to the Chairperson of a proposed contract where the manager has a beneficial interest with the supplier. A disclosure to the developer at the first meeting which is followed up by a minute does not comply with section 122A(3) because:
    1. No notice in writing was given to the Chairperson as required by section 122A(3)
    2. At the time the notice is given under section 122A(3) there must be a proposed contract. There was no proposed contract at 11 July 2022. The work order was not created until September 2022. The disclosure must be given in the context of a proposed contract so the owners corporation can make an informed decision about whether it consents to the contract in light of the disclosure. A general disclosure of a relationship does not meet the requirements of the Act.
    3. MOCS was required to disclose the work order to the chairperson before the work order was issued to Leon and did not do so.
    4. The disclosure of the beneficial interest to the developer was not given in good faith, in breach of MOCS’ duty under section 122(1)(a) of the Act. The notice to the developer was a cynical attempt to technically comply with the Act in circumstances where:
      1. The notice to the developer was unlikely to result in an objection by the owners corporation,
      2. MOCS knew that the lot owners who would be paying for the gardening services would not be informed of the beneficial interest,
      3. At no time after settlement and before Leon was employed did MOCS bring the disclosure made in the minutes to the attention of the new owners.
  2. Further, I find MOCS breached section 122(d) of the Act. MOCS has not taken reasonable steps to ensure that the gardening services were procured at a competitive price. I accept Mr Kingma’s evidence that the same services could have been arranged with Jim’s Mowing for $30. Gardeners average an hourly rate of $40 to $60. A charge of up to $50 to mow the nature strip might have been competitive. A charge of $99 to mow a nature strip is not a competitive price. This is a serious breach of the Act. The sum is small, but over the course of a three-year contract, Leon stood to make a substantial sum. The breach is more egregious because of the failure to disclose the beneficial interest.
  3. Had the work order been disclosed to the owners corporation as required, the owners corporation would have rejected it. The owners corporation has proved the claim for refund of $396.
  4. The breach of duty by MOCS under sections 122(1)(a), 122(c) and 122(d) entitled the owners corporation to terminate the contract of appointment. If I am wrong in declaring the meeting of 11 July 2022 void, the owners corporation can rely on the breach of duty to justify termination of the contract, even though it did not rely on the breaches at the time of termination.

The damages claim of $827.26

  1. The contract expired on 10 November 2022. There is no entitlement to damages. The sum must be refunded.

The additional services of $330

  1. On 10 November 2022, MOCS representatives attended the annual general meeting at the home of Mr Kingma. MOCS charged the owners corporation for 90 minutes of additional services to attend the meeting. Mr Kingma said that the charges were excessive and if he knew that the owners corporation would be charged an additional amount, he would not have permitted MOCS to attend. Under the contract of appointment, the annual fee of $1100 is inclusive of various services including attendance at the annual general meeting of up to 2 hours. The meeting on 10 November 2022 was the annual general meeting and there were no additional meetings. MOCS was not entitled to charge for an additional meeting because there was no additional meeting. The $330 must be refunded.

Summary of proven claims

Refund of management fees
$ 216.68
Refund of management fees and disbursements:
$ 396
Refund of damages claim:
$ 827.26
Refund of additional services:
$ 330
Total amount to be refunded to the owners corporation:
$1,769.94
  1. I will allow interest under section 165 (c)(ii) of the Owners Corporations Act 2006 (Vic) from date of issue to date of hearing at the penalty interest rate. The amount is $177.41.

L Rowland

Member
